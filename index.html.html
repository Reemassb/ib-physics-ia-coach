📈 Choosing & Linearising Your Graph
Linear Graph (y = mx + c)
Use when your variables have a direct (linear) relationship. Plot DV on y-axis, IV on x-axis. The gradient gives you the physical constant. Example: F vs. x for Hooke's Law → gradient = spring constant k.
Linearisation: y = ax² → y vs. x²
If theory predicts y ∝ x², plot y against x² to get a straight line. Example: Period² vs. length for a pendulum (T² = 4π²L/g) — gradient = 4π²/g, allowing you to calculate g.
Linearisation: y = a/x → y vs. 1/x
If y is inversely proportional to x, plot y against 1/x. Example: Pressure vs. 1/Volume for Boyle's Law → straight line through origin confirms the relationship.
Linearisation: y = ae^(bx) → ln y vs. x
Take the natural log of the exponential variable. Example: Radioactive decay N = N₀e^(−λt) → plot ln N vs. t → gradient = −λ (decay constant).
IB examiners expect a linearised graph with error bars, a best-fit line, and a gradient calculation with units. Always quote your gradient to 3 significant figures.
± Uncertainty Analysis
📏

1. Instrument Uncertainty (Absolute)

For analogue instruments: ½ the smallest division. For digital instruments: ± the last digit.

e.g. Ruler smallest division = 1 mm → Δx = ± 0.5 mm
🔁

2. Uncertainty from Repeats (Random)

Take at least 3 repeats. Use ½ the range (max − min) as the uncertainty.

Δx = (x_max − x_min) / 2
➕

3. Adding / Subtracting Values

Add the absolute uncertainties.

z = a + b → Δz = Δa + Δb
✖️

4. Multiplying / Dividing Values

Add the fractional (percentage) uncertainties.

z = a × b → Δz/z = Δa/a + Δb/b
🔺

5. Powers

Multiply the fractional uncertainty by the power.

z = aⁿ → Δz/z = n × (Δa/a)
📊

6. Uncertainty from Graph Gradient

Draw the steepest and shallowest lines through the error bars. The uncertainty in the gradient is half the difference between their gradients.

Δm = (m_max − m_min) / 2
Quote all uncertainties to 1 significant figure. Quote the corresponding measurement to the same decimal place.
📐 Statistical Treatment
Mean & Standard Deviation
Always calculate the mean of repeated measurements. If you have 5+ repeats, you can quote the standard deviation as a measure of spread. For IB, showing mean ± SD demonstrates statistical thinking.
Percentage Uncertainty
% uncertainty = (absolute uncertainty / measured value) × 100. Use this to compare the significance of different sources of uncertainty. Any source > 5% deserves discussion in your evaluation.
Comparing with Literature Value
Calculate % discrepancy = |experimental − literature| / literature × 100%. Then assess whether your uncertainty range overlaps with the literature value. If it does, systematic error may be small; if it doesn't, discuss systematic sources.
Pearson Correlation Coefficient (r)
For HL students: r measures how well your data fits a linear trend (r = 1 means perfect positive correlation). Use a spreadsheet to calculate. Quote r² (coefficient of determination) to show how much variation your model explains.
You do NOT need advanced statistics for IB IA. Clear tables, a linearised graph with error bars, and a thoughtful uncertainty analysis are enough for full marks in Analysis.
🔎 Evaluation: What Examiners Want
State a Conclusion with Evidence
Link your result directly to your RQ. State whether the data supports or contradicts the hypothesis, and quote numerical evidence. Don't just say "the graph shows a straight line" — state what the gradient means physically.
Identify Systematic vs. Random Errors
Systematic errors shift all values in one direction (e.g. a zero error on a ruler). They show up as a non-zero y-intercept or a constant offset. Random errors cause scatter around the best-fit line. Distinguish between them — examiners value this distinction highly.
Suggest Specific Improvements
Generic suggestions (e.g. "do more repeats") score 0. Specific ones score marks: "Replace the manual stopwatch with a photogate timer to reduce human reaction time uncertainty from ±0.2 s to ±0.001 s."
Extensions
Suggest a genuinely new investigation that builds on yours — different variable, different range, or different physical setting. Show you understand the broader physics context of your work.
The Evaluation criterion is where most students lose marks. Spend as much time on it as on data collection.
